Household Safety Notes · December 2026

Holiday scam season: 6 quick checks for your household

Hi [Client first name],

December brings more packages, more online orders and more texts about both. Scammers know it. Below are six checks that take a few minutes each and make your household a harder target this month. None of them cost anything.

By the numbers
People reported losing $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025, from about 3 million reports to the Federal Trade Commission.1 The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged just over 1 million complaints and $20.9 billion in reported losses in 2025; people 60 and over reported about $7.7 billion of that.2 The FTC notes that many losses are never reported.

1. "Your package couldn't be delivered." Don't tap the link.

Fake delivery texts copy the Postal Service, FedEx, UPS and others. The link leads to a look-alike site that asks for a small "redelivery fee" and takes your card number. If you're expecting a package, check its status on the site or app where you ordered it, not through the text. Report the text with your phone's "report junk" option or forward it to 7726 (SPAM).3,4

2. Anyone who asks to be paid with a gift card is a scammer

That's the FTC's rule of thumb, and it has no exceptions: no real business or government agency asks you to buy a gift card and read them the numbers on the back. If it already happened, call the card company right away using the number on the back of the card, ask for a refund, and keep the card and the receipt.5

3. Pick a family code word for "emergency" calls

Some scammers now use voice-cloning tools to sound like a relative who "needs money right now and please don't tell anyone." The FBI's 2025 report counted more than 22,000 complaints involving AI, with close to $900 million in reported losses.2 Agree on a word only your family knows. If a call or message asks for urgent money, hang up and call the person back on the number you already have for them.5

4. Shop on sites you typed in yourself

Deals in ads and social posts can lead to copycat stores. Type the store's address yourself or use its official app, look closely at the web address before you check out, and pay with a credit card rather than a debit card. Credit cards generally come with stronger legal limits on what you owe for fraudulent charges, and a debit card draws straight from your bank account.6

5. Turn on two-step sign-in for email and shopping accounts

Your email account can reset the password on almost everything else, so protect it first. Turning on multifactor authentication (a code or an app prompt on top of your password) means a stolen password alone isn't enough to get into the account. Then do the same for the stores you buy from most.7

6. Add new gifts to your home inventory

New electronics, jewelry and tools are easy to forget if you ever need to list what you own. Take a photo of each item, note the brand, model, serial number and price, and keep the receipt. The free NAIC Home Inventory app can walk you through it, room by room. Keep a copy somewhere other than your house.8 If you're unsure how your current policy treats high-value items, that's a good question to bring to us.

If something already went wrong
Report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and online crimes to the FBI at ic3.gov. Contact your bank or card company right away about any payment you didn't authorize. Forward scam texts to 7726.
